# Balance Exercises for Men Over 60: The 10-Minute Routine

Five exercises, about ten minutes, at least three days a week. All of it happens at the kitchen counter, with a hand within reach of the edge.

Balance starts slipping in the mid-50s and keeps slipping. Among adults 65 and over, one in four reports a fall each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury at that age, according to the CDC. Balance responds to training at any age. In the Cochrane review of 108 trials in people 60 and over living at home, exercise cut the rate of falls by 23%, and balance and functional exercises on their own cut it by 24%.

Most of the people in those trials were women: 77%. The home program this routine borrows from, New Zealand's Otago program, checked its results in men separately. It cut falls and fall injuries by 35%, and it worked as well in men as in women.

Your feet are part of the balance system. The soles tell your brain where your weight is, and the toes press into the floor to catch a sway. In a year-long study of older adults, the ones who fell had weaker toes and more disabling foot pain than the ones who didn't. When a podiatry program for older adults with foot pain added foot and ankle exercises, orthotics, and shoe advice, falls dropped by 36%.

## Start with the 10-second test

Stand next to the counter. Lift one foot and rest the top of it against the back of the other calf. Arms by your sides, eyes on a point at eye level. Hold for 10 seconds without touching down. You get three tries per foot.

In a study of 1,702 adults aged 51 to 75, one in five couldn't do it. Over a median of seven years, 17.5% of those who couldn't hold the stance died, against 4.6% of those who could. After accounting for age, sex, weight, and health conditions, their risk of death was 84% higher. That's an association: the test picks out who is at risk, and it doesn't prove that training balance adds years. It makes a good yardstick. Write down your result and take the test again every four weeks.

---

## Who this routine is for

- Men over 60, whether or not they've ever fallen.
- Men in their 50s who failed the 10-second test.
- Men who've had an ankle sprain. A sprain dulls the joint's position sense, and the [sprained ankle guide](/guides/sprained-ankle-recovery-over-40) explains why that lasts.
- Men with mild numbness in the feet or with diabetes, once a doctor has checked the cause. The [numbness guide](/guides/numbness-and-tingling-in-the-feet) covers what to rule out.
- Men who've started reaching for the stair rail or who feel unsteady in the dark.

## See a doctor before you start if

- You've fallen in the past year, or you worry about falling. A fall-risk check and a program set by a physical therapist are the better start. Otago itself was prescribed and delivered by trained health professionals.
- You get dizzy when you stand up, roll over in bed, or turn your head.
- Your balance has gotten worse over days or weeks, or numbness in your feet is new or spreading.
- You take medicines that make you drowsy or light-headed. A doctor or pharmacist can check whether any of them raise your fall risk.

## Set up

- **Work at the kitchen counter** or behind a sturdy chair, close enough to grab it. Never in open space.
- **Wear flat shoes with a firm sole.** Skip socks on hardwood or tile. In one study, older adults who fell at home were far more likely to go barefoot or in socks around the house, so shoes indoors are worth keeping on after the routine too.
- **Clear the floor** of rugs, cords, and anything you could step on.

---

## The routine

Five exercises in this order. About ten minutes.

### 1. One-leg stand

Face the counter with your fingertips on the edge. Lift one foot, keep your hips level and your standing knee soft. Hold 10 seconds, then switch.

**3 holds per side.** Build toward 30 seconds per hold.

This is the core of the routine. Your ankle, hip, and the small muscles of the foot all work to keep you over a small base.

### 2. Heel-to-toe walk

Walk along the counter, placing one heel directly in front of the other foot's toes. Keep your eyes forward, not on your feet. Touch the counter if you need to.

**10 to 20 steps.** Turn and walk back.

A narrow base forces the sideways control you need on a curb or a narrow path.

### 3. Sideways walk

Stand with your feet together and your knees slightly bent. Step sideways slowly with one foot, then bring the other to meet it. Keep your hips level.

**10 steps each way.**

It trains the hip muscles that stop a sideways sway, the direction the heel-to-toe walk doesn't cover.

### 4. Sit-to-stand

Use a firm chair without wheels. Cross your arms over your chest, stand up without using your hands, then sit down slowly to a count of three.

**10 reps.** If you can't stand without your hands yet, push off the armrests and work toward it.

Leg strength is the other half of fall prevention. In the Cochrane review, programs that combined balance work with strength exercises cut the rate of falls by 34%.

### 5. Heel and toe raises

Hold the counter. Rise onto the balls of your feet and lower slowly. Then rock back onto your heels and lift your toes.

**10 of each.**

The fallers in the foot study had stiffer ankles and weaker toes. This keeps both working.

**Total time: about 10 minutes.**

## How often

At least three days a week. Balance work is light, so unlike the [strength routine](/routines/strength) it doesn't need rest days between sessions; daily is fine. The NHS sets two days a week of strength and balance work as the minimum for adults over 65.

The trials that cut falls the most had two features in common. They challenged balance, and they added up to more than three hours of exercise a week; together those cut falls by 39%. Ten minutes three times a week covers the balance work. Walking, the strength routine, or a tai chi class covers the rest. Tai chi on its own cut falls by 19% in the Cochrane review.

---

## Weekly progression

**Weeks 1 and 2.** Both hands on the counter for everything. Holds of 10 seconds. Sit-to-stand with your hands if you need them.

**Weeks 3 and 4.** One fingertip on the counter. One-leg holds toward 20 seconds. Sit-to-stand with your arms crossed.

**Weeks 5 to 8.** Hands hovering an inch above the counter. One-leg holds of 30 seconds. Heel-to-toe without touching. Swap the sideways walk for the grapevine: cross one foot over the other as you step sideways, 5 cross-steps each way. The smaller the step, the harder the balance work.

**After week 8.** Close your eyes on the one-leg stand, hands hovering over the counter. Or stand on a folded towel. Take the 10-second test again. Then move the work into the day: a one-leg stand while you brush your teeth, heel raises while the coffee brews.

If an exercise gets harder instead of easier, drop back a step for a week.

## Common mistakes

**Practicing in open space.** Stay within a hand's reach of the counter at every stage, including eyes closed.

**Watching your feet.** Keep your eyes forward. You look ahead when you walk, and that's the balance you're training.

**Staying on the easy version.** Balance improves when it's challenged. If a hold is easy for three sessions in a row, move to the next step.

**Doing it in socks.** Socks on a smooth floor can slide mid-hold.

**Treating it as a substitute for a fall check.** After a fall, see a doctor first, even if you weren't hurt.

---

## When to escalate

- Any fall, even one without an injury. Tell your doctor. In the Otago trials, men and women who had fallen in the year before went on to fall at more than twice the rate of those who hadn't.
- Dizziness or a spinning feeling when you turn your head, stand up, or roll over.
- Balance that keeps getting worse despite practice.
- New or spreading numbness in your feet.
- Diabetes plus any change in balance or foot sensation.

Any of those, book a visit. The [doctor-prep checklist](/doctor-prep) keeps it focused.

**Call 911** for a sudden loss of balance with weakness or numbness on one side, a drooping face, slurred speech, or sudden trouble walking. Those are signs of a stroke.

## Bottom line

Ten minutes at the kitchen counter, at least three days a week, harder every few weeks. Take the 10-second test now and again every four weeks. Pair the routine with regular walking and the [strength routine](/routines/strength).
