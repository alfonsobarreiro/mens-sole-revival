# Home Remedies and Laser for Toenail Fungus: What the Evidence Says

Search "toenail fungus cure" and the first page is vinegar soaks, tea tree oil, Vicks VapoRub, baking soda, Listerine, garlic, and a laser clinic ad. Some of these have been studied. Most haven't. This guide goes through the shelf in order of evidence, says what each one has been shown to do, and tells you which are harmless to try alongside real treatment and which are a year wasted.

Two things before the list. First, none of these has been shown to cure toenail fungus at anything like the rate of the prescription options in [treatments, compared](/guides/toenail-fungus-treatments-compared). Second, "I tried it and the nail looked better" is not evidence, because a filed, cleaned, short nail always looks better, and a nail that was never fungal, which is half of them, was never going to respond to anything. Get the diagnosis first: [is it toenail fungus?](/guides/is-it-toenail-fungus).

---

## Vicks VapoRub

**What's been studied.** One small pilot study, 18 people, applied the ointment daily to fungal toenails for 48 weeks. At the end, 5 of the 18 had a nail that was both clear and fungus-free on the lab test, 10 had partial improvement, and 3 had no change. The active ingredients thought to matter are camphor, menthol, and eucalyptus oil, which have antifungal activity in the lab.

**What that means.** A result worth knowing about: about a quarter cured in a tiny, uncontrolled study, with nobody comparing it to doing nothing. It's cheap and safe on intact skin. It's not a first choice, and for a man who can take the pill it's a poor trade against a treatment that cures nearly twice as many in a controlled trial. Reasonable as an add-on, or for a mild infection at the tip in a man who has decided against prescriptions.

## Tea tree oil

**What's been studied.** One trial from the 1990s compared 100% tea tree oil applied twice daily for six months with a 1% antifungal cream that isn't itself a nail treatment. Both groups improved by about the same amount on looks, and both did poorly on the lab test. A later trial of a tea tree cream combined with an antifungal drug showed some benefit, but the drug may have done the work.

**What that means.** Tea tree oil has antifungal activity in the lab and weak, old evidence on nails. It irritates skin in some people and can cause an allergic rash. If you use it, it's on the nail plate only, not the skin fold, and it's a supporting act.

---

## Snakeroot extract

**What's been studied.** A Mexican plant, Ageratina pichinchensis, in a couple of small trials against ciclopirox lacquer, with roughly similar results. Ciclopirox's own complete cure rate is under 1 in 10, so "similar to ciclopirox" is a low bar. Hard to find in a standardized form outside those trials.

---

## Vinegar and other soaks

**What's been studied.** Nothing on toenails. The idea is that fungus dislikes acid, which is true in a dish. On a nail, a daily 15-minute soak in diluted vinegar doesn't reach the fungus under the plate, and a year of it hasn't been tested anywhere.

**What that means.** Harmless on intact skin, useless as a cure. The soak does soften the nail, which makes filing easier; if you like the ritual, file afterward and count that as the benefit. Bleach soaks, sometimes recommended online, damage skin and aren't worth the risk.

---

## Baking soda, garlic, mouthwash, hydrogen peroxide, oregano oil, coconut oil, ozonated oils

**What's been studied.** Lab-dish activity for several of them, no nail trials for any. Mouthwash and peroxide are also sold on the "it kills germs" logic that fails at the nail plate.

**What that means.** Skip, or treat as harmless if it comes with filing and drying. Don't let any of them stand in for treatment past the first month.

---

## Drugstore "fungal nail" kits

Most contain urea, which softens and thins the nail, or a cosmetic whitener, or a low-strength antifungal that penetrates nail poorly. Urea is useful: a thinner nail responds better to any real treatment, and a 40% urea cream at night for a few weeks does the job. The rest is packaging. Read the ingredients; if the active ingredient isn't a named antifungal drug at a nail-treatment concentration, it isn't a nail treatment.

---

## Laser

**What's been studied.** More than the home remedies, and the picture is still poor. The devices heat the nail to damage the fungus. The regulatory clearance is for a "temporary increase in clear nail", which is a cosmetic claim. The most careful review of the trials found them small, short, and inconsistent, with low certainty of any benefit, and clearance rates well below oral treatment. A few better-run studies show some improvement when laser is combined with a topical, but the topical may be doing the work.

**The cost.** Several hundred to over a thousand dollars for a course, almost never covered by insurance.

**What that means.** If you can't take the pill and the topical alone has failed, laser plus topical is a defensible thing to try with a podiatrist who is honest about the odds. On its own, for the price, it's the worst value on this page.

---

## What all of this shares

Every remedy above works better in the stories than in the studies for the same reasons: the nail gets filed, cleaned, and trimmed as part of the ritual, which improves the look; the skin dries out because someone is paying attention; and in half the cases there was no fungus to begin with. Those are real benefits. They're just not the remedy.

If you want to use a home remedy, do it like this:

1. **Diagnosis first.** A nail clipping test at the podiatrist.
2. **Alongside, not instead.** Prescription treatment does the killing; the remedy is company.
3. **Filing and drying every time.** That's the part that helps.
4. **Photos monthly.** If the clear band at the base isn't growing by month three, the remedy isn't working, and the [12-month protocol](/guides/toenail-fungus-12-month-protocol) explains what should be.
5. **Stop at the first sign of skin irritation.** Redness or blistering around the nail means the remedy is doing harm.

---

## Bottom line

Vicks has one small, promising, uncontrolled study. Tea tree oil has old, weak evidence and a rash risk. Snakeroot matches a weak prescription. Vinegar, baking soda, garlic, mouthwash, and peroxide have no nail evidence at all. Urea thins the nail and helps real treatment; that's the one drugstore ingredient worth buying. Laser is expensive and unproven on its own. Get the diagnosis, take the treatment with the best odds, and keep any remedy in the supporting role.
