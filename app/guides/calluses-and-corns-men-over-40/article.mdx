# Calluses and Corns: What to Remove, What to Leave, and What's Causing Them

You've been filing the same hard patch under the ball of your foot for years, and it comes back every few weeks. Or a corn on a toe catches on every dress shoe you own, and the drugstore pad burned the skin around it without touching the corn.

Same pattern in both cases. A callus is skin answering pressure or friction: your body thickens the skin where something pushes too hard, too often. Shave it without moving the pressure and it comes back on schedule. Men over 40 get more of them for reasons that are mostly mechanical and mostly fixable.

This piece covers what a callus is versus a corn, how to read where one forms, the order to fix it in, what to leave alone, and when a podiatrist should handle it.

If you're not sure whether the pain is skin or something deeper, the [5-minute self-check](/assessment) sorts that out first.

---

## What a callus is, and what a corn is

Both are the same tissue: thickened outer skin (hyperkeratosis) built up against repeated pressure or rubbing. It's protective until it grows dense enough to press on the tissue underneath, at which point the protection is what hurts.

**A callus** is a broad plate of thick skin with no clear edge, usually under the ball of the foot or along the heel. It aches more than it stabs.

**A hard corn** is small and dense, with a central core of compressed keratin pointing inward. It sits on or beside a toe joint, or under a metatarsal head, and pressing straight down on it hurts.

**A soft corn** forms between toes, most often the fourth and fifth, where one toe's bone rubs the neighbor's. Sweat keeps it white and rubbery.

Why more after 40: the fat pad under the ball of the foot thins, so bone sits closer to shoe. Toes drift and curl (hammertoes), putting a joint where a shoe upper never expected one. The shoes that fit at 30 don't fit a foot that has widened. Add a standing job and a gait that shifted around a stiff big toe or ankle, and load lands where it didn't used to.

---

## The map: where it forms tells you why

**Under the second metatarsal head.** The commonest spot. That bone is carrying load the big toe should, because the big toe is stiff or the shoe crowds it. [The ball-of-foot guide](/guides/ball-of-foot-pain-in-men-over-40) covers the mechanism.

**Heel edge.** The heel pad spreading under load in a loose or open-backed shoe. Same root as [cracked heels](/guides/cracked-heels-what-actually-works).

**Inner side of the big-toe joint.** A bunion rubbing the shoe upper, or the big toe rolling inward at push-off. Fit, nearly every time.

**Top of a curled toe.** A hammertoe joint hitting the roof of a shallow toe box, usually a [dress shoe](/guides/what-your-dress-shoes-are-doing-to-your-feet).

**Between the fourth and fifth toes.** Two toe bones pressed together by a narrow forefoot, whatever the size on the box says.

## The self-check

**Press straight down, then squeeze from the sides.** A corn hurts most under direct downward pressure. A plantar wart hurts more when pinched from the sides and usually shows tiny black dots. A plain callus is dull either way.

**Look for a core.** With the skin soft after a shower, check the center of the spot. Dense and slightly translucent means a corn; an even plate with no center means a callus.

**Check the matching shoe.** Pull the insole out of the shoe you wear most. A shiny or dented spot that lines up with the callus shows where the load goes.

**The diabetes rule, in one line:** if you have diabetes, nerve damage in your feet (neuropathy), or poor circulation, callus care is a podiatrist's job, not a pumice stone's, because a callus can hide an open sore (ulcer) underneath that you can't feel.

## What to do, in order

Skin work without pressure work is maintenance forever.

### 1. Change the pressure

Start with the shoe the callus matches. Get measured at the end of the day and buy for the wider foot. A toe box that lets the toes lie flat removes the cause of most toe corns. For a callus under the ball of the foot, a metatarsal pad placed just behind the metatarsal heads, or an insole with support built in ([Superfeet Blue](/reviews/superfeet-blue-insoles) is the one the site has tested), spreads load off the spot. For a hard corn on a toe, a silicone toe sleeve stops the rubbing; for a soft corn, a small spacer keeps the bones apart. A donut-shaped moleskin pad takes load off a painful corn in the meantime. Socks count too: a heavy toe seam adds friction; a flat-seamed cushioned sock doesn't.

Pain usually eases within one to two weeks of moving the pressure, before the skin has changed much.

### 2. Thin the skin, safely

After a shower or a few minutes in warm water. Pumice stone or foot file, never a blade. A few strokes across the thick area, then stop; you're taking the top layers off, not the whole callus. Pink means you've gone too far. Once or twice a week is enough.

Every night, a urea cream on the spot; urea softens thickened skin so the file has less to do. [Gehwol Fusskraft Soft Feet](/reviews/gehwol-fusskraft-soft-feet-cream) is the one the site reviews. Cotton socks over it help.

With both in place, a callus under the ball of the foot thins noticeably in three to four weeks, and a hard corn usually loses its core in four to six weeks once the rubbing stops. Move nothing upstream and it rebuilds in about the same time.

## What to leave alone

**Never cut it with a blade.** Razors, callus knives, and nail scissors go deeper than you can see, and a cut under thick skin heals slowly and infects easily.

**Skip medicated corn pads if you have diabetes or poor circulation.** The acid in them (salicylic acid) dissolves skin without knowing where the corn ends. On healthy skin it mostly burns the ring around the corn; on a foot with poor sensation or blood flow it can open a wound that becomes an ulcer.

**Don't remove all of it.** A thin, even layer over a bony spot is doing its job. File it flush with the surrounding skin and leave it.

---

## When to see a podiatrist

**A corn that bleeds, weeps, has red skin spreading from it, or has a black or discolored spot under it needs a podiatrist this week, not next month.**

Book when:

- A callus is still painful to walk on after four weeks of the pressure-plus-skin protocol.
- You can't reach or see your feet well enough to check them.
- You have diabetes, neuropathy, or circulation problems, whatever the callus looks like.

A podiatrist can pare it down with a sterile blade in one visit, check whether a bony bump or hammertoe is driving it, and fit padding or an orthotic that moves the load. Bring the [doctor-prep checklist](/doctor-prep) so the visit covers the cause, not just the skin.

---

## What doesn't work, or works less than you'd think

**Bathroom surgery.** The core grows back within weeks, and some men trade a corn for an infection.

**Callus shavers used aggressively.** Two or three passes work. Going until the skin is pink restarts the thickening response.

**Skipping the shoe change.** The most common reason a callus returns. Filing and cream without a change in load is a subscription.

**One-off pedicures.** A pedicure thins the skin for about two weeks. Same shoe on the walk home, same callus by the next appointment.

---

## Bottom line

A callus or a corn is a pressure map of your foot. Find the shoe or the joint causing the load and change that first. Then thin the skin with a file and a nightly urea cream, and leave a protective layer behind.

Most calluses in men over 40 respond in four to six weeks once the pressure is gone. The ones that don't, and any callus on a foot with diabetes or poor circulation, belong to a podiatrist. The [weekly routine](/routines/weekly) keeps the skin in check once the load is handled.
