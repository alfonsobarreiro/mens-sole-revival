## What the arch actually does

{/* Section 1 (~200 words).
    Prompt: quick anatomy pass — the arch is a spring made of bone,
    ligament, and muscle. The plantar fascia is the passive ligament
    bowstring; the intrinsic muscles are the active suspension; the
    tibialis posterior tendon is the medial stabilizer. All three work
    together. When any one weakens, the others compensate — but only
    for so long. Sustained walking loads them all.

    No aphorisms; the "spring" analogy is enough. */}

## Why arches hurt after walking (three common causes)

{/* Section 2 (~300 words). Explicitly three sub-buckets. */}

### 1. Weak intrinsics under a fine structure

{/* Prompt: most common cause in men over 40 who have been in
    supportive shoes for decades. The arch itself is fine; the small
    muscles have atrophied from disuse. When you walk enough to load
    them, they fatigue and ache. Diagnostic clue: pain is symmetric
    (both feet), builds gradually over the day, eases fast with rest.

    This is the case the strength protocol fixes. */}

### 2. Footwear over-support (the crutch pattern)

{/* Prompt: highly cushioned or heavily arch-supported shoes do the
    intrinsics' job. Muscles never contract. Over years, they weaken.
    When you walk far enough that fatigue kicks in — or switch to a
    less supportive shoe — the arch aches.

    Diagnostic clue: pain first appeared after changing to less
    supportive shoes, OR appears specifically when barefoot at home.

    Cross-link: [[what-your-dress-shoes-are-doing-to-your-feet]] for
    the parallel argument on footwear compression. */}

### 3. Actual arch collapse (posterior tibial dysfunction)

{/* Prompt: less common but worth ruling out. Progressive collapse
    of the medial arch usually starts on one foot (asymmetric), gets
    worse over months not days, and often comes with visible flattening
    of the arch when viewed from the front. Pain is often on the
    inside of the ankle, not the arch itself.

    Diagnostic clue: asymmetric, progressive, ankle-medial pain.
    Send to a podiatrist. Do not attempt to strengthen through this
    one. */}

## Self-check: which one is you?

{/* Section 3 (~250 words). Structured checklist. */}

{/* Prompt:
      · Wet-foot test (step on paper after a bath — visible flat sole
        vs preserved arch print). Genuine collapse shows a flat print.
      · Single-leg squat (stand on one foot, slowly lower into a
        quarter squat while looking at your knee in a mirror). Knee
        that caves inward = arch collapsing under load.
      · Towel scrunch (seated, foot flat, scrunch a towel under toes).
        If you can't feel the arch muscles engage, intrinsics are
        weak. If it feels effortful, they're not developed.

    Interpret: preserved wet print + can't do towel scrunch → cause
    1 (weak intrinsics; strength protocol below). Preserved wet print
    + knee-cave in single-leg → cause 2 (footwear over-support; add
    strength AND transition slowly out of over-supportive shoes).
    Flat wet print + asymmetric + ankle pain → cause 3 (see podiatrist). */}

## The short-foot activation protocol (2 minutes, daily)

{/* Section 4 (~300 words).
    Prompt: this is the foundational drill for weak intrinsics.

    Seated, feet flat, without curling the toes, draw the ball of the
    foot toward the heel to shorten the foot and raise the arch. Hold
    5 seconds. Release. 10 reps per side.

    Common mistake: curling toes to fake it. If you can only do it by
    curling, cue this — try to keep the big toe flat while shortening.

    Progress:
      · Week 1: seated, 10 reps per side, 2x per day.
      · Week 2: standing (harder, small weight bearing).
      · Week 3: single-leg standing.
      · Week 4: single-leg standing while doing something else
        (brushing teeth).

    Cross-link: [[5-minute-routine]] for anchoring this to an
    existing habit. */}

## Progressive load: the walking build

{/* Section 5 (~250 words).
    Prompt: once the short-foot protocol is comfortable, the real
    test is progressive walking load. Rule: increase weekly walk
    volume by ~10% max. If arches ache the day after a walk,
    reduce next week's target.

    If pain persists past 8 weeks of consistent short-foot work +
    graded walking, escalate to a podiatrist to rule out cause #3
    (tibial dysfunction). */}
