## Why plantar fasciitis is different after 40

{/* Section 1 (~250 words).
    Prompt: three specific things change with age that reshape the
    exercise protocol.
      1. Calf muscles + Achilles stiffen faster overnight; morning
         stretch matters more.
      2. Recovery slows; single load session should be short (not
         30-minute PT sessions).
      3. Fat pad under the heel has thinned; barefoot on hard floor
         during flare-ups adds insult. Progress into it only when pain
         is <2/10.

    Cite: NIH StatPearls PF (peak 40–60). */}

## The morning stretch protocol (3 moves, ~3 minutes)

{/* Section 2 — this is the anchor. Write per H3.
    Prompt overall: these are in-bed / bedside stretches to do BEFORE
    the first step of the day. That's the diagnostic moment for morning
    PF pain, so it's the highest-leverage stretch window.

    Structure: each H3 = one stretch with numbered steps. This maps
    well to HowTo schema if we add it later; write in step form. */}

### 1. Towel calf stretch (in bed)

{/* Prompt: seated in bed, legs extended, loop a towel around the ball
    of one foot, gently pull toes toward you until you feel a stretch
    in the calf and along the arch. Hold 30 seconds. Switch sides.
    Repeat once per side.

    Voice note: describe the sensation to expect ("stretch in the
    calf, not sharp pain"). Cue for common mistakes ("don't yank"). */}

### 2. Plantar fascia stretch (bedside)

{/* Prompt: bedside, cross one ankle over the opposite knee. Pull the
    toes back toward the shin with one hand; press thumb along the
    arch with the other, walking from heel to ball, feeling for tight
    spots. 30 seconds per side.

    Cue: this is the specific fascia stretch, not the calf. */}

### 3. Standing calf stretch (before the first step)

{/* Prompt: hands against wall or bed frame, one leg back with heel
    down and knee straight (gastrocnemius), then repeat with the back
    knee bent (soleus). 20 seconds each variation, both sides. Total ~
    80 seconds.

    Cue: bent-knee version targets the soleus which is often the
    tighter of the two in men over 40. */}

## The strength protocol (3 moves, 3x per week)

{/* Section 3 — the load side. Structure same as above. */}

### 1. Single-leg calf raises (heel over the edge)

{/* Prompt: stand on a step with heel hanging off the back. Slowly
    lower the heel below step-level, then rise onto the ball. 10 reps
    per side, 2 rounds. Progress to slow eccentric (3-second lower)
    once easy. This is the highest-evidence single exercise for PF
    per current PT literature.

    Cite: PMC eccentric calf raise for PF (search + insert citation
    Alfonso — placeholder to add: e.g. Rathleff 2015 Scand J Med Sci
    Sports "High-load strength training improves outcome in patients
    with plantar fasciitis") */}

### 2. Towel scrunch (intrinsic activation)

{/* Prompt: seated, feet flat on floor, place a towel under one foot,
    scrunch toes to gather it toward you. 10 reps, 2 rounds. Wakes up
    the intrinsic foot muscles that offload the plantar fascia. */}

### 3. Short-foot activation (arch drill)

{/* Prompt: seated with foot flat, without curling the toes, "shorten"
    the foot by drawing the ball toward the heel to raise the arch.
    Hold 5 seconds, release. 10 reps, 2 rounds. This is the specific
    intrinsic-only activation cue; the ability to isolate this is
    itself a diagnostic (many men over 40 initially can't).

    Cross-link: [[big-toe-and-your-whole-body]] on the intrinsic /
    windlass connection. */}

## Weekly progression: when to add, when to hold

{/* Section 4 (~300 words).
    Prompt: give a plain load progression rule. Simple version:

      · Weeks 1–2: morning stretch daily + strength 2x/week. Pain
        may briefly increase in week 1 as tissue re-loads. That's
        expected; if pain is > 5/10, back off strength (stretches only).
      · Weeks 3–4: strength 3x/week. Add the eccentric heel-drop
        variation.
      · Weeks 5+: if pain is <3/10, add barefoot walking on soft
        surfaces (rug, grass) for 5 minutes daily. This is the
        real-world load test.

    Rule: pain should not exceed 3/10 during exercise, and should
    return to baseline within 24 hours. If either fails, back off.

    Voice note: no "listen to your body" clichés. State the pain
    threshold rule plainly. */}

## What not to do

{/* Section 5 (~200 words).
    Prompt:
      · Aggressive foam-rolling of the arch during acute inflammation
        (can worsen).
      · Barefoot walking on hard floors during flare-ups (fat pad
        can't absorb it).
      · Skipping the morning stretch because it "isn't hurting today"
        (it prevents the next morning's spike).
      · Doing strength work every day (no adaptation window; needs
        rest days). */}

---

## When to add a night splint

{/* Section 6 (~200 words).
    Prompt: if morning pain hasn't improved by week 3–4 of consistent
    stretching, a soft night splint (or a rigid boot) keeps the fascia
    at length overnight. That converts the morning "cold stretch" into
    an already-warmed one. Evidence is moderate but positive.

    Point: don't reach for the splint on day 1. Layer it in only when
    the stretch protocol alone isn't clearing morning pain. */}

## Timeline: what to expect

{/* Section 7 (~200 words).
    Prompt:
      · Week 1: pain briefly increases as tissue re-loads.
      · Week 2: baseline pain steady or slightly lower.
      · Week 4: morning pain noticeably shorter (< 2 min vs 5–10 min).
      · Week 8: most men have <2/10 morning pain if consistent.
      · Week 12: most cases resolved OR clearly not-PF and time to
